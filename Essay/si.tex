\section{Event catalogue and sample selection}
\label{sec:si_events}

Table~\ref{tab:events} lists the 16~events included in the main
analysis, ordered by hazard type and then by descending~$\aemp$.
Events were selected from the Facebook Disaster Maps archive using
the following quality criteria: (i) at least three consecutive
post-peak time steps showing monotone decay ($n_{\mathrm{mono}} \geq 3$),
so that a power-law slope can be estimated; (ii) a clearly identifiable
peak in $D(t)$ with no ambiguity arising from multiple activation
periods or overlapping events; and (iii) sufficient tile coverage within
the 200-km analysis disk ($\geq 3$ populated tiles per annulus at peak).

fewer than two near-field tiles were excluded.  Four events with comparatively low goodness-of-fit
($R^2 < 0.75$; marked with $\dagger$ in the $R^2$ column) are
retained in the primary analysis but shown with open markers in all
scatter plots.

\section{PDE model: derivation, Bessel decomposition, and parameter
search}
\label{sec:si_pde}

\subsection*{S3.1 Governing equation}

We model the post-peak evolution of the radial displacement field
$\delta(r,t)$ by a diffusion--relaxation equation in cylindrical
coordinates under radial symmetry:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\delta}{\partial t}
    = \frac{\Ds}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}
      \!\left(r\,\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial r}\right)
    - k\,\delta, \quad 0 \leq r \leq R,
  \label{si:eq:pde}
\end{equation}
where $\Ds$~[km$^2$\,h$^{-1}$] is a lateral diffusion coefficient
and $k$~[h$^{-1}$] is a uniform first-order relaxation rate.  The
domain radius $R = 200$~km matches the analysis window.

\noindent\textbf{Boundary conditions.}
Neumann (zero-flux) conditions are imposed at both boundaries:
\[
  \left.\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial r}\right|_{r=0} = 0, \qquad
  \left.\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R} = 0 .
\]

\subsection*{S3.2 Analytical Bessel-series solution}

Because $\delta(r,t)$ satisfies a separable linear PDE with Neumann
boundaries on a disk, the general solution is a superposition of
Bessel-$J_0$ modes~\citep{Crank1975}:
\begin{equation}
  \delta(r,t) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n\,J_0\!\left(\frac{\mu_n r}{R}\right)
    e^{-\lambda_n (t-t_{\mathrm{peak}})},
  \label{si:eq:bessel}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mu_n$ are the non-negative zeros of $J_0'(x) = -J_1(x)$
        ($\mu_0 = 0$, $\mu_1 = 3.832$, $\mu_2 = 7.016$, \ldots;
        \textbf{note}: $\mu_n$ here denotes Bessel zeros, distinct from the
        empirical decay rate~$\aemp$ used throughout the paper),
  \item $N = 10$ modes (convergence verified; adding modes
        $n > 10$ changes $\apred$ by $< 0.3\%$),
  \item $\lambda_n = k + \Ds\,(\mu_n/R)^2$ is the modal decay rate.
\end{itemize}

The key physics: higher-order modes (larger $\mu_n$, finer spatial
structure) decay exponentially faster than the fundamental mode.
An initial profile rich in high-order components therefore relaxes
faster in aggregate than a smooth, low-mode profile.

\subsection*{S3.3 Projection of observed profiles onto Bessel basis}

At $t = t_{\mathrm{peak}}$, the expansion coefficients $\{c_n\}$
are determined by $r$-weighted least squares from the observed
10-km-binned radial profile $\hat\delta(r_i)$:
\begin{equation}
  \{c_n\} = \arg\min_{\{c_n\}} \sum_i r_i\,
  \left[\hat\delta(r_i)
        - \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n\,J_0\!\left(\frac{\mu_n r_i}{R}\right)
  \right]^2 .
  \label{si:eq:lsq}
\end{equation}
The weight $r_i$ reflects the area of each annular bin and ensures
that outer bins (larger $r$, more tiles) do not numerically dominate
the fit.

\subsection*{S3.4 Computation of $\apred$}

Rather than tracking $\delta(r,t)$ directly, we use an energy-type
metric:
\begin{equation}
  E(t) = \frac{\int_0^R [\delta(r,t)]^2\,r\,\mathrm{d}r}
               {\int_0^R r\,\mathrm{d}r}
       \;\approx\; \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n^2\,e^{-2\lambda_n
               (t-t_{\mathrm{peak}})}
  \quad\text{(Parseval approximation)}.
  \label{si:eq:energy}
\end{equation}
$E(t)$ is the $L^2$ energy norm, whereas the empirical $D(t)$ is an
$L^1$ mean-absolute-deviation.  Both summarize aggregate relaxation,
but $E(t)$ has an exact Parseval representation that facilitates
interpretation.  We fit $\log E$ vs.\ $\log(t - t_{\mathrm{peak}})$
by OLS over $[1,\,120]$~h to obtain~$\apred$.  The starting point
$1$~h (versus $24$~h for~$\aemp$) is chosen to capture the rapid
initial decay of high-order modes; the model's role is to predict the
\emph{ranking} of recovery rates, not their absolute magnitude.

\subsection*{S3.5 Global parameter estimation}

Parameters $k$ and $\Ds$ are shared across all 16~events.  We search
a log-uniform $30 \times 30$ grid ($k \in [10^{-4},\,10^{0}]$~h$^{-1}$,
$\Ds \in [10^{-2},\,10^{1}]$~km$^2$\,h$^{-1}$), then refine with a
$30 \times 30$ sub-grid around the coarse optimum (grid spacing reduced
by $10\times$).  The joint optimisation criterion minimises the sum of
three normalised rank statistics:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(k, \Ds)
    = \mathrm{rank}\!\left[-\rho_{\mathrm{S}}(\apred,\aemp)\right]
    + \mathrm{rank}\!\left[-r(\apred,\aemp)\right]
    + \mathrm{rank}\!\left[\mathrm{MAE}(\apred,\aemp)\right] ,
  \label{si:eq:criterion}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{\mathrm{S}}$ denotes Spearman rank correlation,
$r$~Pearson correlation, and MAE is mean absolute error.  The optimal parameters under this criterion are:
\[
  k^* = 0.00418\;\mathrm{h}^{-1}, \quad
  \Ds^* = 0.304\;\mathrm{km}^2\,\mathrm{h}^{-1} .
\]

\subsection*{S3.6 Counterfactual experiments}

Three counterfactual conditions test the necessity of diffusion and
spatial profile structure (Table~\ref{tab:counterfactual}):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No diffusion} ($\Ds = 0$): all modes collapse to the
        same rate~$k$; $E(t) \propto e^{-2kt}$ is profile-independent,
        so $\apred$ is identical for all events and
        $\rho(\apred,\dnear) = 0.0$.
  \item \textbf{$c_0$-only}: only the uniform (zero-wavenumber) mode
        is retained; the spatially heterogeneous structure of the
        profile is erased, again yielding $\rho(\apred,\dnear) = 0.0$.
  \item \textbf{Shuffled profiles}: the observed profile of event~$j$
        is assigned to event~$k$ at random (500~permutations).
        The median $\rho = -0.008$ with 95\% CI $[-0.518,\,+0.519]$
        confirms that the signal requires each event's \emph{own}
        spatial profile.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{S3.7 Bootstrap robustness of $\rho(\apred, \dnear)$}

To assess sensitivity to the observed radial profiles, we perturb
each event's profile by multiplicative Gaussian noise ($\pm 10\%$)
and refit $\{c_n\}$ for 500~realisations.  The resulting bootstrap
distribution of $\rho(\apred,\dnear)$ has:
\begin{itemize}
  \item median $\rho = -0.482$,
  \item 95\% CI $= [-0.763,\,-0.079]$,
  \item 98.4\% of realisations yield $\rho < 0$.
\end{itemize}
The mechanism is robust to profile measurement uncertainty.

\section{Distance-segment robustness of the shape--rate correlation}
\label{sec:si_distance}

To test whether the shape--rate signal is driven by a specific radial
zone, we compute $\rho(\aemp,\bar\delta_{\mathrm{zone}})$ separately
for near-field ($r < 50$~km), mid-field (50--100~km), and far-field
($100$--$200$~km) segments.  $\bar\delta_{\mathrm{zone}}$ is the
spatial mean of the displacement field within each zone at
$t_{\mathrm{peak}}$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Near-field} ($r < 50$~km, $n = 16$):
        $\rho = -0.526$, $p = 0.036$.
  \item \textbf{Mid-field} (50--100~km, $n = 9$ events with
        sufficient tiles):
        $\rho = -0.733$, $p = 0.025$.  The stronger correlation
        reflects the sharper spatial gradient in the evacuation
        shadow.  The reduced sample ($n = 9$) limits
        generalisation.
  \item \textbf{Far-field} ($100$--$200$~km, $n = 16$):
        $\rho = -0.20$, $p = 0.46$ (not significant).
        Far-field displacement is predominantly diffuse and
        weakly correlated with recovery rate.
\end{itemize}

The primary predictor $\dnear$ (near-field average) is chosen because
it maximises sample coverage while remaining physically interpretable
as the net population flux near the disaster epicenter.

\section{Additional robustness results}
\label{sec:si_additional}

\subsection*{S6.1 Leave-one-out jackknife}

Jackknife resampling (leave-one-out, $n=16$ iterations) yields a
95\% CI for $\rho(\aemp,\dnear)$ that does not cross zero: the
leave-one-out replicate distribution spans $[-0.62,\,-0.44]$, confirming that
no single event drives the correlation.

\subsection*{S6.2 High-goodness-of-fit subsample}

Restricting to events with $R^2 \geq 0.8$ ($n = 11$), the
correlation strengthens: $\rho = -0.673$, $p = 0.023$.  This
indicates that measurement noise from imperfect power-law fits
attenuates the observed association; the true signal is plausibly
stronger.

\subsection*{S6.3 Sensitivity to $r_{\mathrm{near}}$ and
$R_{\max}$}

Varying the near-field radius $r_{\mathrm{near}}$ from 10 to 200~km
shows a broad plateau of significant negative $\rho$ (peak at
$\approx 50$~km), confirming the result is not threshold-sensitive.
Varying the domain radius $R_{\max}$ from 50 to 400~km yields
qualitatively identical results for $100 \leq R_{\max} \leq 250$~km;
the primary analysis uses $R_{\max} = 200$~km.

\subsection*{S6.4 Residual displacement vs.\ decay rate}

The residual displacement $D_\infty$ (mean of normalised $D(t)$
over the post-peak tail) is strongly anticorrelated with~$\aemp$:
$\rho(\aemp, D_\infty) = -0.703$, $p = 0.002$.  Events with
faster decay also reach lower residual levels, consistent with the
interpretation that $\aemp$ captures genuine recovery rather than a
transient fluctuation.

\subsection*{S6.5 Excluded candidate predictors}

The following variables were tested as potential predictors of
$\aemp$ but produced $|\rho| < 0.15$ and $p > 0.5$ in bivariate
analysis: spatial concentration index of the initial displacement
profile, recovery gradient ($\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}t$ at
$t_{\mathrm{peak}}$), and observation window length.  None was
retained in the main analysis.

\subsection*{S6.6 Hazard-type grouping test}

To assess whether recovery speed varies systematically by hazard type,
we apply a Kruskal--Wallis rank-sum test to the five hazard groups
(earthquake $n=2$; hurricane $n=6$; typhoon $n=1$; flood $n=6$;
wildfire $n=1$).  The test yields $H = 2.61$, $p = 0.63$, indicating
no significant difference.  Merging hurricane and typhoon into a
single tropical-cyclone category and restricting to groups with
$n \geq 2$ (earthquake, flood, tropical cyclone) gives $H = 0.51$,
$p = 0.77$.  In both cases, within-group variation in~$\aemp$ far
exceeds between-group variation, confirming that hazard type alone does
not explain recovery speed diversity.
